# TEARDOWN-FORMAT.md

The deliverable template for a website teardown. This is the structure of the final document — not a checklist to fill in mechanically, but a format that ensures the analysis is useful and scannable.

---

## Document structure

### Verdict (2-3 sentences)

The site's essential character in three sentences or fewer. Lead with the strongest move and the biggest miss. End with who the site is really for (which may differ from who it claims to be for).

**Example verdicts:**

> Linear's strongest move is the design itself — every pixel communicates "this is a tool built by people who care about craft," which is the core selling point to its audience. Its biggest miss is the pricing page, which buries the per-seat cost math and doesn't address the "what happens when my team grows" question. The site is for technical leads at funded startups who equate visual craft with engineering quality.

> This site's strongest move is the specificity of its social proof — every testimonial names a metric and a timeframe, which builds cumulative credibility through the scroll. Its biggest miss is the first impression: the hero headline could apply to any product in the category, so all that credibility is wasted on visitors who bounce before scrolling. The site is for mid-market ops managers who are already comparing solutions, not for people discovering the category.

**What makes a good verdict:**
- Names the specific strong/weak dimension (not "the design is good" — "the design communicates X which matters because Y")
- Identifies the real audience (which might differ from the stated audience)
- Creates a mental model the reader can carry through the rest of the document

### First impression

What you understood in 5 seconds. Structured as:

- **I understood:** [what was immediately clear]
- **I assumed:** [what the design/imagery communicated without the copy]
- **I couldn't tell:** [what remained unclear after the initial scan]
- **The hierarchy led me:** [where the eye went: first, second, third]

This section should be 3-5 bullet points maximum. It's a snapshot, not an essay.

### Dimension scores

A table for at-a-glance orientation. Scores are 1-5. The one-line note justifies the score — it's not a summary of the full analysis, just enough to orient the reader.

| Dimension | Score | Note |
|-----------|-------|------|
| First impression | X/5 | [one line] |
| Information architecture | X/5 | [one line] |
| Copy & messaging | X/5 | [one line] |
| Visual design | X/5 | [one line] |
| Conversion design | X/5 | [one line] |
| Trust & credibility | X/5 | [one line] |
| Technical signals | X/5 | [one line] |
| What's missing | X/5 | [one line] |

**Scoring guidance:**
- **5** — Best-in-class. You'd use this as a reference example.
- **4** — Strong. Clearly intentional and effective. Minor opportunities exist.
- **3** — Acceptable. Functional, standard, unremarkable. Neither helps nor hurts.
- **2** — Weak. Noticeable problems that likely affect outcomes. Below category standard.
- **1** — Broken. Actively harmful to the site's goals. Immediate attention needed.

Most dimensions for most sites will score 3. A score of 5 or 1 should be rare and notable.

### Standout patterns (2-4 items)

Things the site does notably well. These are the lessons worth studying — regardless of whether the rest of the site is strong or weak. For each:

- **What:** The specific pattern (not "good design" — "the testimonial placement adjacent to CTAs creates a trust-conversion loop")
- **Why it works:** The underlying principle that makes it effective
- **Specifics:** The exact implementation details that make it work (the typeface choice, the copy structure, the layout decision)

### Missed opportunities (2-4 items)

Things that are weak or missing, with what good would look like. These are not complaints — they're constructive observations about where the site leaves value on the table. For each:

- **What's happening:** The current state (specific, observable)
- **Why it's a miss:** What the visitor experiences or concludes as a result
- **What good would look like:** A specific alternative, grounded in what works elsewhere in the category or on this site itself

### Transferable lessons (if applicable)

When the user is tearing down for inspiration, call out patterns that can be adapted to their context. For each lesson:

- **The pattern:** What the site does (structure, not surface)
- **Why it's transferable:** What makes it work independent of this specific context
- **How to adapt it:** What would change when applied to the user's product/audience

Only include this section when the user is explicitly looking for inspiration or learning. Omit for pure competitive analysis.

### Competitive implications (if applicable)

When the user is tearing down a competitor, call out what the teardown reveals about positioning, audience, and gaps. Structure:

- **What they're claiming:** The positioning stance (explicit and implicit)
- **Where they're investing:** What the site emphasizes (features, trust, design, content)
- **Where they're vulnerable:** Dimensions where they're weak or dimensions they're ignoring
- **What this means for you:** Positioning gaps to exploit, claims to counter, audiences they're underserving

Only include this section when the user is explicitly doing competitive analysis. Omit for self-analysis or inspiration.

## Tone guidance

Teardowns should be analytical, not mean. The goal is to make decisions legible — to explain why something works or doesn't — not to score points for cleverness.

**Do:**
- "The hero headline doesn't work because it could apply to any product in the category — it doesn't differentiate."
- "The pricing page builds trust through transparency — every cost is visible, with no surprises at checkout."
- "The design communicates 'enterprise' through its conservative color palette and formal typography, which may or may not match the actual target audience."

**Don't:**
- "The hero is terrible."
- "Whoever wrote this copy clearly didn't try."
- "This looks like it was designed in 2015."

Even strong sites have weaknesses. Even weak sites have lessons. The teardown's job is to find both, explain both, and make both useful.

---

## Length guidance

A single-site teardown should be 400-800 words in the final document. Long enough to be substantive, short enough to be read. The verdict and dimension scores should orient a reader in 30 seconds. The full document should take 3-5 minutes to read.

If the analysis for any dimension exceeds 100 words, you're going too deep for the format. Save detailed analysis for follow-up — the teardown document is a map, not the territory.
