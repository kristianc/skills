# COMPARATIVE-TEARDOWN.md

How to tear down multiple sites together for competitive analysis. A comparative teardown reveals category patterns, positioning gaps, and differentiation opportunities that single-site analysis cannot.

---

## When to use

- Comparing 3-5 competitors in a category before positioning your own product
- Evaluating a category you're entering to understand the established conventions
- Identifying what everyone in a space does the same (category conventions) vs. what's differentiated
- Preparing for competitive-positioning skill (this feeds directly into that process)

**Not for:** Tearing down more than 5 sites at once (diminishing returns — you're mapping the whole market, not analyzing decisions). Not for sites in different categories (there's nothing to compare).

---

## Structure: dimension-by-dimension, not site-by-site

The cardinal rule of comparative teardowns: organize by dimension, not by site.

**Wrong structure** (site-by-site):
- Site A: first impression, copy, design, conversion...
- Site B: first impression, copy, design, conversion...
- Site C: first impression, copy, design, conversion...

This forces the reader to hold Site A's typography in memory while reading through Site B's conversion design before they can compare typography. It's unreadable.

**Right structure** (dimension-by-dimension):
- First impression: Site A vs. B vs. C
- Copy & messaging: Site A vs. B vs. C
- Conversion design: Site A vs. B vs. C

This makes patterns and differences visible immediately. The reader sees who's doing what in each dimension without needing to cross-reference.

---

## The comparison matrix

For each dimension, produce a comparison row:

| Dimension | Site A | Site B | Site C | Category pattern | Gap |
|-----------|--------|--------|--------|-----------------|-----|
| First impression | [their approach] | [their approach] | [their approach] | [what's common] | [what no one does] |

**Column definitions:**

- **Site columns:** One sentence describing each site's approach to this dimension. Specific, not evaluative. "Enterprise tone, feature-led hero, dark palette" — not "good" or "bad."
- **Category pattern:** What's common across all or most sites. This is the convention — the thing visitors in this category expect because everyone does it.
- **Gap:** What no one in the category does well, or what no one does at all. This is where differentiation opportunity lives.

## Identifying category conventions vs. differentiators

### Category conventions

Things that every (or nearly every) site in the category does. These are:

- Expected by visitors (breaking them causes confusion, not differentiation)
- Table stakes (having them doesn't differentiate, but lacking them disqualifies)
- The "floor" — you must meet this standard to be taken seriously in the category

**How to identify:** If 3+ of 5 sites do the same thing, it's a convention. Note it, but don't celebrate anyone for doing it — and flag anyone who doesn't.

**Examples:** A developer tool category where every site has: dark mode support, code examples in the hero, "Docs" as the first nav item, terminal-style typography accents.

### Differentiators

Things that only one (or at most two) sites do. These are:

- Choices that signal a specific positioning or audience
- Deliberate departures from category convention
- Potential competitive advantages (or disadvantages, if the category convention exists for a reason)

**How to identify:** If only 1-2 of 5 sites do something, it's a differentiator. Evaluate whether it's a strength (intentional, effective) or an oversight (everyone else does it for a reason).

**Examples:** In a category where every competitor leads with features, one site leads with customer outcomes and social proof. That's a differentiator — they're betting on credibility over capability claims.

## Finding positioning gaps

A positioning gap is something that:
1. Matters to the target audience (it's not trivial)
2. No one in the category does well (or does at all)
3. Could be claimed by your product (it's achievable, not fantasy)

### How to find them

1. **List what everyone claims:** If every competitor says "fast," "easy," and "powerful," those claims are undifferentiated. Nobody owns them.
2. **List what no one claims:** Look at the gaps column in the comparison matrix. What dimensions does the category collectively ignore?
3. **List what the audience needs that isn't addressed:** What questions would a buyer have that no site in the category answers well?
4. **Cross-reference with your product's actual strengths:** Which of the gaps align with things you genuinely do well?

### Common gap types

- **Trust gap:** Nobody in the category invests in social proof, transparency, or risk reduction. Opportunity to win on credibility.
- **Clarity gap:** Everyone uses jargon and assumes category knowledge. Opportunity to win on accessibility to newcomers.
- **Design gap:** Everyone looks the same (same template, same layout, same palette). Opportunity to win on visual distinctiveness.
- **Specificity gap:** Everyone makes vague claims. Opportunity to win on concrete, verifiable outcomes.
- **Audience gap:** Everyone targets the same persona. Opportunity to win by serving an underserved buyer within the category.

## The comparative verdict

The final synthesis for a comparative teardown should answer:

1. **What does this category look like?** (The conventions, the expected standard)
2. **Who's leading and why?** (The strongest site and what makes it strong)
3. **Where is everyone weak?** (The category-wide gaps)
4. **What's the opportunity?** (The positioning space that's open)

This should be 3-5 sentences — a strategic summary that feeds directly into competitive-positioning.

---

## Feeding into other skills

A comparative teardown produces inputs for:

- **competitive-positioning:** The gaps and differentiators become positioning axes to evaluate
- **ai-design-polish:** The category's visual conventions become reference points for the user's own design
- **trust-building-patterns:** The trust gap analysis reveals which trust signals are underused in the category
- **improve-product-feel:** The category's craft standard becomes the bar to meet or exceed
