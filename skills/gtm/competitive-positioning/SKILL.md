# Competitive Positioning

Define where the product wins — not on every axis, but on axes that matter to the target customer and that competitors can't or won't match.

Positioning is not a tagline exercise. It's the strategic decision about which fight to pick. The deliverable is a positioning document that makes the competitive landscape legible, identifies the axes of real differentiation, and gives the product a claim it can defend.

## Glossary

Use these terms exactly. Full definitions in GLOSSARY.md.

- **Positioning** — the deliberate choice of which axes to win on and which to concede.
- **Wedge** — the intersection of an axis the customer cares about, the product is strong on, and competitors are structurally weak on.
- **Structural constraint** — an architectural, business model, or market decision that durably limits what a competitor can do.
- **Claim** — a specific, verifiable statement about the product's strength on an axis.
- **Proof point** — a testable fact that supports a claim.
- **Competitive frame** — the narrative of how the product relates to each alternative.
- **Category** — the mental shelf the customer puts the product on.

## Principles

- **Positioning is a choice about what to give up.** A product positioned for everyone is positioned for no one. The document names what the product is NOT for.
- **Claims must be verifiable.** "Best-in-class" is not positioning. Something a prospect could test is. See POSITIONING-DOCUMENT.md for the test.
- **Only position against structural constraints.** A bug they'll fix next quarter is not an opportunity. An architectural decision that limits what they can offer is. See RESEARCH-FRAMEWORK.md for how to distinguish.
- **The customer's alternative is not always a competitor.** Spreadsheets, manual processes, and doing nothing are all alternatives.

## Process

### 1. Define the field

Establish with the user: who is the target customer (role, company size, situation — specific, not broad), what alternatives does this customer consider (direct competitors, adjacent tools, manual processes, status quo), and what is the customer's current pain (their lived experience, not the product's value prop).

Ask the user. Don't infer the field.

### 2. Research competitors

For each alternative, research what they claim, what they actually deliver, their pricing model, their structural constraints, and who they're optimized for. Use web search for current data — never rely on memory for competitor details. Full methodology in RESEARCH-FRAMEWORK.md.

### 3. Map the axes

Identify every axis customers use to evaluate options in this category. For each axis, map where each competitor and the user's product falls. Common axes and how to evaluate them in AXES.md. Be honest — the point is to find where the product genuinely wins, not to manufacture advantages.

### 4. Find the wedge

The wedge is the intersection of an axis the target customer cares deeply about, where the product is genuinely strong, and where competitors are structurally constrained. Present candidate wedges to the user. For each: the axis, why the customer cares, why the product wins, why competitors can't follow, and what it costs. See AXES.md for what "winning" on an axis means and what it costs.

Ask the user: "Which of these feels right? Which would you bet on?"

### 5. Write the positioning document

Once the user picks the wedge, produce the positioning document: category, target customer, key differentiator, proof points, competitive frame, and what the product gives up. Format and guidance in POSITIONING-DOCUMENT.md.

## Rules

- Never position against a competitor's temporary weakness. Only against structural constraints.
- Every claim must be currently true, not aspirational. "Will have" is not positioning.
- If the product doesn't have a clear wedge, say so. The skill's job is to find one or surface that it doesn't exist yet — not to manufacture false differentiation.
- Competitive research must use current data. Search the web. Don't hallucinate competitor features.
- Include "do nothing / status quo" as an alternative. It's often the real competitor.
