# Competitive Positioning

Define where the product wins — not on every axis, but on axes that matter to the target customer and that competitors can't or won't match.

Positioning is not a tagline exercise. It's the strategic decision about which fight to pick. The deliverable is a positioning document that makes the competitive landscape legible, identifies the axes of real differentiation, and gives the product a claim it can defend.

## Principles

- **Positioning is a choice about what to give up.** A product positioned for everyone is positioned for no one. The document should make clear what the product is NOT for.
- **Claims must be verifiable.** "Best-in-class" is not positioning. "Sub-200ms p95 latency on queries under 10M rows" is. Every claim should be something a prospect could test.
- **Competitors' weaknesses are only useful if they're structural.** A bug they'll fix next quarter is not a positioning opportunity. An architectural decision that limits what they can offer is.
- **The customer's alternative is not always a competitor.** Spreadsheets, manual processes, hiring an intern, and doing nothing are all alternatives. Include them.

## Process

### 1. Define the field

Establish with the user:

- **Who is the target customer?** — Role, company size, situation. Be specific: "Series A SaaS founders evaluating their first analytics tool" not "companies that need analytics."
- **What alternatives does this customer consider?** — Direct competitors, adjacent tools, manual processes, status quo.
- **What is the customer's current pain?** — Not the product's value prop — the customer's lived experience before they start looking.

### 2. Research competitors

For each alternative identified, research and document:

- **What they claim** — positioning copy from their homepage, product pages, and sales materials. Exact quotes.
- **What they actually deliver** — based on documentation, changelogs, public reviews, community forums, and any direct experience. Where does the claim hold up? Where does it fall apart?
- **Pricing model** — how they charge, what's included, what's gated.
- **Structural constraints** — architectural decisions, market positioning, or business model choices that limit what they can build or offer. These are the durable weaknesses.
- **Who they're optimized for** — which customer profile gets the best experience. This reveals who they're NOT optimized for.

Use web search for current data. Don't rely on memory for competitor details — positioning based on stale information is positioning based on fiction.

### 3. Map the axes

Identify every axis customers use to evaluate options in this category. Common ones:

- Price
- Speed / performance
- Ease of setup
- Depth of functionality
- Integration breadth
- Support quality
- Data ownership / privacy
- Customizability
- Brand / trust / longevity

For each axis, map where each competitor (and the user's product) falls. Be honest — the point is to find where the product genuinely wins, not to manufacture advantages.

### 4. Find the wedge

The wedge is the intersection of:

- **An axis the target customer cares deeply about**
- **Where the product is genuinely strong**
- **Where competitors are structurally weak** (not temporarily behind — structurally constrained)

Present the candidate wedges to the user. For each:

- **The axis** — what dimension of evaluation
- **Why the customer cares** — what pain or desire this connects to
- **Why the product wins** — specific, verifiable capability or characteristic
- **Why competitors can't follow** — the structural constraint that makes this durable
- **What it costs** — what the product gives up by leaning into this axis (there's always a cost)

Ask the user: "Which of these feels right? Which would you bet on?"

### 5. Write the positioning document

Once the user picks the wedge (or refines it), produce:

**Category** — what the product is. One sentence. This is the mental shelf the customer puts it on.

**Target customer** — who it's for, specifically. Include who it's NOT for.

**Key differentiator** — the wedge, stated as a claim. Verifiable, specific, defensible.

**Proof points** — 3-5 specific facts, features, or characteristics that support the claim. Each one testable.

**Competitive frame** — how the product relates to each major alternative. Not a feature matrix — a narrative. "Unlike X, which optimizes for Y, we optimize for Z because our customers need..."

**What we give up** — explicit statement of what the product sacrifices for its positioning. This is the honesty check. If nothing is given up, the positioning is weak.

## Rules

- Never position against a competitor's temporary weakness. Only position against structural constraints.
- Every claim in the positioning document must be currently true, not aspirational. "Will have" is not positioning.
- If the user's product doesn't have a clear wedge, say so. The skill's job is to find one or surface that it doesn't exist yet — not to manufacture false differentiation.
- The competitive research must use current data. Search the web. Don't hallucinate competitor features.
- Include "do nothing / status quo" as an alternative. It's often the real competitor.
