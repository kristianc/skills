# SCORING-FRAMEWORK.md

How to evaluate and prioritize candidate segments. The framework uses eight dimensions scored 1-5, but the value is in the rigor of each score, not the arithmetic of the total.

## The eight dimensions

### 1. Value fit

**What it measures:** Does the product solve this segment's primary or secondary problem, or is it a nice-to-have they could live without?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | The product solves the segment's #1 problem. Without it, they're meaningfully worse off. They would notice within a week if it disappeared. |
| 4 | The product solves their #2 problem, or solves #1 but with gaps they work around. They'd notice within a month. |
| 3 | The product addresses a real problem but isn't the primary or secondary priority. They'd eventually notice it was gone. |
| 2 | The product is a nice-to-have. They'd shrug if it disappeared. |
| 1 | The product solves a problem this segment doesn't actually have, or solves it so partially that they need another tool anyway. |

**Data sources:** Customer interviews, support tickets (what do they contact you about — the core value or peripheral features?), usage data (what features do they actually use?), churn reasons (did they leave because the core value was insufficient, or for unrelated reasons?).

**Common trap:** Scoring based on what the segment *could* get from the product if they used it fully, rather than what they *actually* get. Value fit is about the real experience, not the theoretical maximum. A segment that theoretically benefits enormously but in practice only uses 20% of the product scores 2-3, not 5.

### 2. Reach

**What it measures:** Can you find, contact, and sell to this segment through channels you have or can build?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | The segment is concentrated in channels you already use effectively. You know where they are, how to reach them, and have proven messaging that works. |
| 4 | The segment is reachable through channels you have but haven't optimized for them. You know where they are but haven't proven you can convert them. |
| 3 | The segment is reachable but diffuse — spread across many channels, no single concentration point. Acquisition will be more expensive. |
| 2 | The segment is hard to identify from outside. You can describe them but can't easily build a list or target them in advertising. |
| 1 | The segment is invisible until they self-identify. No reliable way to proactively reach them. |

**Data sources:** Current acquisition channel performance, cost per acquisition by segment, LinkedIn/database coverage of target titles, community and content presence of the segment, conference and event landscape.

**Common trap:** Scoring reach based on where the segment exists in the abstract rather than whether *you* can reach them. Enterprise buyers exist on LinkedIn — but if your sales team has no enterprise sales experience and no ABM infrastructure, reach is lower than the channel's theoretical potential.

### 3. Willingness to pay

**What it measures:** Is the pain acute enough that the segment will pay what you need to charge, and does the value justify the price?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | The pain is acute, alternatives are expensive or nonexistent, and the segment routinely pays at or above your price point for comparable tools. Budget is not a gating issue. |
| 4 | The segment will pay your price but considers it carefully. They compare alternatives and negotiate, but close at your range. |
| 3 | The segment will pay, but only at the lower end of your range. They have budget but see significant alternatives, including free/cheap ones. |
| 2 | The segment is price-sensitive. They'll push for discounts, compare heavily on price, and may choose inferior alternatives to save money. |
| 1 | The segment expects this category to be free or very cheap. Your price point is outside their mental model for this kind of tool. |

**Data sources:** Current deal sizes and discount rates by segment, competitor pricing in the segment, the segment's reference prices (what adjacent tools do they pay for?), budget ownership and approval processes, churn correlated with pricing.

**Common trap:** Confusing willingness to pay with ability to pay. A well-funded enterprise might be *able* to pay $100K/year but *willing* to pay only $30K because their reference price for this category is anchored by a $30K competitor. WTP is shaped by value perception and alternatives, not just budget.

### 4. Speed to value

**What it measures:** How quickly can a customer in this segment reach the activation moment — the point where they first experience the core value?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Same day. The product delivers core value within hours of signup. Minimal setup, no integrations required, no behavior change needed. |
| 4 | First week. Requires some setup or data import, but a motivated customer gets to value without hand-holding. |
| 3 | First month. Requires meaningful onboarding — integrations, configuration, training, or process change. Works with reasonable effort. |
| 2 | First quarter. Requires significant implementation — professional services, data migration, workflow redesign, organizational change management. |
| 1 | Six months or more. The product requires a transformation project before value is realized. High risk of abandonment before activation. |

**Data sources:** Time-to-activation metrics by segment, onboarding completion rates, professional services engagement rates, customer feedback on setup difficulty, support tickets in the first 30 days.

**Common trap:** Measuring speed to setup rather than speed to value. A customer can complete onboarding in a day but not experience core value for a month if the product requires data to accumulate, habits to form, or a workflow trigger to occur. Speed to value is measured from the customer's perspective: when do they first think "this was worth buying"?

### 5. Retention potential

**What it measures:** Once activated, does the product become more valuable over time, or does the value plateau or decay?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Strong compounding. The product becomes more valuable the longer you use it — accumulated data, network effects, embedded workflows, institutional knowledge captured. Switching cost increases over time. |
| 4 | Steady value. The product continues to deliver consistent value. The customer has no reason to leave, though switching costs are moderate. |
| 3 | Value holds but doesn't grow. The product does what it does, but there's no deepening. Vulnerable to a competitor that offers the same thing cheaper or better. |
| 2 | Value decays. The initial problem gets solved or diminishes, and the product becomes less relevant. Think: migration tools, one-time setup tools used as subscriptions. |
| 1 | One-shot. The product delivers value once. Ongoing retention requires finding new value to deliver. |

**Data sources:** Cohort retention curves by segment, usage trends over time (increasing, flat, declining), expansion revenue rates, feature depth adoption (do they use more features over time or stay shallow?), qualitative churn reasons.

**Common trap:** Assuming retention from contract length. A 12-month contract doesn't mean the customer is retained — it means they haven't had a chance to leave yet. Look at engagement and usage trends, not contract status.

### 6. Expansion potential

**What it measures:** Can the account grow in spend after initial purchase?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Multiple expansion vectors. More users, more use cases, more departments, higher tiers. Average account grows 50%+ in the first year. The initial purchase is a beachhead. |
| 4 | Clear expansion path. More users or higher usage tiers. Average account grows 20-50% in the first year. |
| 3 | Some expansion possible. A few additional seats or a tier upgrade, but growth plateaus relatively quickly. |
| 2 | Limited expansion. The initial purchase is close to the full potential. Maybe one upsell opportunity. |
| 1 | No expansion. The initial purchase is the ceiling. Revenue growth requires new logos, not account expansion. |

**Data sources:** Net revenue retention by segment, expansion revenue as percentage of initial deal, seat/usage growth over time, cross-sell and upsell conversion rates, account penetration (what % of eligible users/teams are using the product?).

**Common trap:** Scoring expansion based on the theoretical org chart rather than actual buying behavior. A 10,000-person company *could* expand from 10 seats to 1,000 — but if in practice, accounts in this segment plateau at 50 seats, score against reality.

### 7. Support cost

**What it measures:** Can you serve this segment at a cost that makes unit economics work?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Self-serve. The segment rarely contacts support. When they do, issues are straightforward and resolve quickly. Documentation and in-product guidance are sufficient. |
| 4 | Light touch. Occasional support needs, but they're standard and efficient to resolve. No custom work required. |
| 3 | Moderate support. Regular support interactions, some requiring escalation. Onboarding requires human involvement. Manageable at scale with a support team. |
| 2 | High touch. Frequent support needs, complex issues, custom configuration requests. Requires dedicated customer success. Unit economics work only at higher price points. |
| 1 | Unsustainable. The segment's support needs exceed what the price can cover. Requires custom development, dedicated resources, or constant hand-holding. Every customer is a net cost. |

**Data sources:** Support ticket volume and resolution time by segment, customer success time allocation by segment, professional services revenue and cost by segment, escalation rates, feature request volume and alignment with roadmap.

**Common trap:** Ignoring indirect support costs. A segment might not file many tickets but require extensive sales engineering, custom integrations, or security reviews. Count the full cost of serving the segment, not just the support queue.

### 8. Strategic fit

**What it measures:** Does serving this segment well make the product better for the next customer like them — and for the broader customer base?

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 5 | Perfect alignment. What this segment needs is exactly what the product should be building. Features built for them serve the entire customer base. They are the atomic unit the product is designed around. |
| 4 | Strong alignment. Most of what this segment needs aligns with the product roadmap. A few segment-specific requests, but they're edge cases. |
| 3 | Partial alignment. About half of what this segment needs is on the roadmap. The other half is segment-specific and would need to be weighed against other priorities. |
| 2 | Weak alignment. This segment needs significant features or capabilities that don't serve other segments. Serving them well means building things that only they use. |
| 1 | Misalignment. This segment's needs would pull the product in a direction that harms the experience for other segments. They want a different product. |

**Data sources:** Feature requests by segment and their overlap with the roadmap, integration requests and their generalizability, how the segment uses the product versus how it was designed to be used, competitive landscape for the segment (are you the right category of product for them?).

**Common trap:** Scoring strategic fit based on hopes rather than reality. "If we serve enterprises, we'll build features that also help mid-market" is a hypothesis. Score based on evidence: have features built for similar customers in the past actually served the broader base, or did they become dead code?

## How to weight dimensions

**Default weighting: equal.** Start with all dimensions weighted equally. This forces explicit conversation about trade-offs rather than letting one dimension silently dominate.

**When to adjust weights:**

- **Early-stage product (pre-PMF):** Weight value fit, speed to value, and retention higher. You need to prove the product works before worrying about reach or expansion.
- **Growth-stage product:** Weight reach, willingness to pay, and expansion potential higher. You've proven value; now you need to capture it efficiently.
- **Mature product entering new segment:** Weight strategic fit and support cost higher. You can't afford to build a second product inside the first.

**How to adjust:** Multiply scores by weights before summing. Use weights of 1 (normal), 1.5 (important), or 2 (critical). Don't go below 0.5 or above 2 — if a dimension deserves less than 0.5 weight, it's not relevant and should be dropped from the analysis.

State the weights explicitly in the deliverable. Hidden weighting produces results no one can interrogate.

## When a single low score should override

Some dimensions are gating: a sufficiently low score should disqualify the segment regardless of total.

**Hard gates (score of 1 is disqualifying):**
- **Value fit = 1.** If the product doesn't solve a real problem for the segment, nothing else matters. You'll churn every customer you acquire.
- **Reach = 1.** If you can't find or contact the segment, you can't sell to them. Revenue is zero regardless of value.

**Soft gates (score of 1-2 should trigger serious discussion):**
- **Support cost = 1.** Serving the segment may be a net loss. Only proceed if pricing can be set high enough to cover the cost — and even then, consider whether the support burden distracts from serving better-fit segments.
- **Strategic fit = 1.** Serving the segment actively harms the product for other customers. This is rarely worth doing regardless of revenue potential.
- **Willingness to pay = 1.** The segment won't pay what you need to charge. Unless you have a clear monetization strategy (freemium funnel, data value, network effects), this is a trap.

## How to handle uncertainty

For early-stage products and new segments, some scores will be uncertain. Handle this honestly:

1. **Score with what you know.** Don't default to 3 (the middle) when you're uncertain. Score your best estimate and mark the confidence: "Value fit: 4 (high confidence — based on 15 customer interviews)" vs. "Expansion potential: 3 (low confidence — no data on account growth yet)."

2. **Note the assumptions.** For any score based on assumptions rather than data, state the assumption explicitly. "Willingness to pay: 4, assuming reference prices anchor on [category] at [$X/month]. If they anchor on [free alternative], this drops to 2."

3. **Identify what would change the score.** For uncertain scores, name what data would move the score up or down. This turns uncertainty into a research agenda rather than a blind spot.

4. **Don't average away uncertainty.** Two segments might have the same total score, but if one has all 3s (known) and the other has 5s and 1s (risky), they are not equivalent. The high-variance segment is a bet; the even segment is a baseline. Name this in the discussion.
