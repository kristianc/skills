# Ideal Customer Profile

Define who the product is for — not in vague demographic terms, but in terms of who gets disproportionate value from what the product actually does today.

An ICP is not a persona with a name and a stock photo. It's a set of qualifying and disqualifying criteria specific enough that anyone in the company can look at a prospect and say "this is us" or "this isn't us." The goal is to stop wasting effort on customers who will churn, and concentrate on customers who will expand.

## Principles

- **Start from the product, not the market.** The ICP describes who gets the most value from what exists, not who you wish would buy it.
- **Disqualifying criteria are as important as qualifying ones.** Knowing who to say no to prevents the slow death of serving everyone poorly.
- **ICPs are testable.** Every criterion should be something you can evaluate from publicly available information, a discovery call, or a product signup — not something that requires months of relationship to learn.
- **One product can have multiple ICPs.** But they should be prioritized, and the primary ICP should be clear enough to drive all defaults (pricing, messaging, feature priority, support level).

## Process

### 1. Audit current value delivery

Before defining who the product is for, understand who it's already working for. Establish with the user:

- **Who are the best current customers?** — Highest retention, highest expansion, lowest support cost, most enthusiastic. If the product is pre-launch, who are the most engaged beta users or the most excited prospects?
- **What do they have in common?** — Not demographics — situations. What problem were they solving? What were they using before? What about their situation makes this product disproportionately valuable?
- **Who are the worst current customers?** — Highest churn, most support tickets, longest sales cycles, most feature requests that don't align with the roadmap. What do they have in common?
- **What does the product actually do well today?** — Not the vision — the current reality. Where is the product genuinely strong, and where is it held together with duct tape?

If the user doesn't have customers yet, work from the product's capabilities and the problem it solves. The questions become: who has this problem most acutely? Who has the fewest alternatives? Who would get value fastest?

### 2. Identify candidate segments

From the patterns above, identify 3-5 candidate customer segments. For each:

- **Situation** — what's happening in their world that creates the need. Not "they need analytics" — "they've outgrown spreadsheet reporting and their first full-time data hire just started."
- **Trigger** — what event makes them start looking. A hire, a funding round, a broken process, a compliance requirement, a competitor move.
- **Current solution** — what they're doing today. Competitor product, manual process, internal tool, nothing.
- **Value the product delivers** — specific, not generic. What changes for them in week 1? Month 1? Quarter 1?
- **Why they'd leave** — what would make this customer churn. This reveals whether the product can actually retain them.

Present the candidate segments and ask the user to react. Which feel right? Which are surprising? Which are the ones they've been avoiding?

### 3. Score and prioritize

For each candidate segment, evaluate:

| Criterion | Question |
|-----------|----------|
| **Value fit** | Does the product solve their #1 or #2 problem, or a nice-to-have? |
| **Reach** | Can you find and contact them through channels you have? |
| **Willingness to pay** | Is the pain acute enough that they'll pay what you need to charge? |
| **Speed to value** | Can they get to the activation moment in days, not months? |
| **Retention potential** | Once activated, does the product become more valuable over time, or is it a one-shot? |
| **Expansion potential** | Can the account grow (more seats, more usage, more products)? |
| **Support cost** | Can you serve them at a cost that makes the unit economics work? |
| **Strategic fit** | Does serving them well make the product better for the next customer like them? |

Score each 1-5. The segment with the highest total is the strongest ICP candidate, but discuss trade-offs — a segment that scores 5 on everything except reach might be worse than one that scores 4s across the board.

### 4. Define the ICP

For the primary segment (and optionally secondary), produce:

**Qualifying criteria** — observable attributes that indicate fit. Each one should be evaluable from public information or a 15-minute discovery call.

- Company characteristics (size, stage, industry, tech stack, growth rate)
- Situation characteristics (what's happening that creates the need)
- Buyer characteristics (role, authority, budget ownership)
- Timing characteristics (what trigger event just happened or is about to)

**Disqualifying criteria** — observable attributes that indicate misfit. Be specific.

- Situations where the product won't deliver value (too small, too large, wrong tech stack, wrong stage)
- Needs the product can't meet (and shouldn't try to meet)
- Signals of likely churn (using it for the wrong job, no internal champion, buying for compliance checkbox only)

**Narrative** — a paragraph describing this customer in human terms. Not a persona with a name — a description of a situation. "A Series B SaaS company that just hired their first data team lead. They've been running on Metabase and spreadsheets, and the new hire is being asked to build real reporting. They need something they can set up this quarter, not a six-month data warehouse project."

### 5. Validate

Ask the user to test the ICP against:

- Their best 3 customers (or most promising prospects) — do they match the qualifying criteria?
- Their worst 3 customers (or most skeptical prospects) — do they match the disqualifying criteria?
- The last 3 deals they lost — which criteria did those prospects violate?

If the ICP doesn't predict past outcomes, it needs revision. Iterate in conversation until it does.

## Rules

- Never define an ICP in purely demographic terms. "Mid-market SaaS companies" is a market segment, not an ICP. Add the situation, trigger, and need.
- Every qualifying criterion must be observable. "Values data-driven decisions" is not observable. "Has a dedicated analytics or data role" is.
- The ICP must include disqualifying criteria. If it doesn't say who's NOT a fit, it's not specific enough.
- Don't build an aspirational ICP. The profile describes who gets value from the product as it exists today, not the product on the roadmap.
- If the user can't identify their best and worst customers, start there. The ICP exercise doesn't work without ground truth.
