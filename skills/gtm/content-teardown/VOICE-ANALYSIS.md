# VOICE-ANALYSIS.md

How to detect, evaluate, and diagnose voice in written content. Voice is what makes writing recognizable — it's how you write, distinct from what you write about. Tone is the attitude in a specific piece; voice is the consistent personality across pieces.

---

## Detecting AI-generated content

AI-generated content has identifiable patterns. These aren't proof — a human can exhibit any of these — but they're signals, and when multiple signals cluster, the probability rises.

### Structural tells

- **The three-paragraph-then-list structure:** Introduction, context, thesis, then a numbered or bulleted list. Over and over. AI defaults to this structure because it's safe and organized, but real writing is messier and more varied.
- **Uniform paragraph length:** Every paragraph is 3-4 sentences. Human writing has more variation — a one-sentence paragraph for emphasis, a longer paragraph when complexity requires it.
- **Perfect symmetry:** Every section has the same number of subsections. Every list has the same number of items. Every example follows the same pattern. Real writers are lumpy — they spend more time on what interests them.

### Language tells

- **Hedging openers:** "It's important to note that..." "It's worth mentioning..." "It should be noted that..." These are filler that delays the actual point. A human with a point makes it.
- **False balance:** "On the other hand..." when there's no genuine counterpoint, or when the counterpoint is immediately dismissed. AI does this to seem balanced; humans with opinions take sides.
- **Generic transitions:** "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally," "In addition" as paragraph openers. These words add nothing — they say "here's another thing" without explaining the relationship between the things.
- **Empty superlatives:** "incredibly powerful," "truly transformative," "absolutely essential," "game-changing." These intensifiers signal that the writer (or model) doesn't have a specific claim to make, so they're substituting volume for precision.
- **Summarizing what was just said:** "In summary," "To recap," "As we've seen," followed by a restatement of the previous section. Human writers trust readers to remember what they just read.
- **The "comprehensive" reflex:** AI tends toward exhaustive coverage rather than selective depth. A post about pricing that dutifully covers every possible pricing model instead of deeply analyzing the one that matters.

### Personality tells

- **No first-person experience:** The content describes what "companies" or "teams" do, never what "I" or "we" did. AI lacks personal experience and defaults to generalized third-person.
- **No humor, vulnerability, or surprise:** The tone is consistently helpful, informative, and neutral. No jokes, no admitted mistakes, no unexpected analogies, no moments where the writer's personality leaks through.
- **No strong opinions without immediate hedging:** "This approach is the best — though of course, every situation is different and what works for one team may not work for another." A human with conviction commits.
- **Perfectly diplomatic disagreement:** When criticizing ideas, every criticism is cushioned. "While this approach has its merits, it may not be the optimal choice in all scenarios." A human who disagrees says why.

## Evaluating voice quality

Voice quality is independent of whether the content is human-written or AI-generated. A human can have a weak voice; an AI-assisted piece can have a strong voice if the human's personality comes through the editing.

### What makes a strong voice

- **Recognizability:** Could you identify this writer without a byline? Strong voice has fingerprints — characteristic word choices, sentence rhythms, types of examples, things the writer always explains and things they never bother to.
- **Consistency:** The voice holds across the piece. It doesn't shift from casual to formal, from confident to hedging, from technical to colloquial without reason.
- **Specificity of opinion:** The writer takes positions and defends them. "I think X because Y" is voice. "Some people think X while others think Y" is the absence of voice.
- **Earned confidence:** The writer's confidence matches their evidence. Overconfidence without evidence is bluster. Under-confidence with strong evidence is false modesty. The match between confidence and warrant is what reads as authentic.
- **Something at stake:** The writer cares about the outcome. They're not summarizing a topic — they're arguing for a position, sharing a hard-won lesson, trying to change how you think about something. You can feel the stakes.

### What makes a weak voice

- **Interchangeability:** Any writer could have written this. Any topic could be swapped in. The content is a container that happens to hold these words, not a specific human's expression of specific thinking.
- **Inconsistent register:** The piece sounds like multiple writers — formal in one section, casual in another, technical in a third. This often happens when different sections are drafted by AI and inconsistently edited.
- **Borrowed authority:** The writer constantly appeals to external authority — "experts say," "research shows," "best practices suggest" — without adding their own perspective. Authority is cited but never earned.
- **Committee voice:** The content sounds like it was written by a group — all edges smoothed, all opinions neutralized, all personality removed in service of a "professional" tone. Safe, forgettable, and indistinguishable from every other company's blog.

## Voice consistency across a piece

Voice consistency is a signal of intentional writing. Check for:

- **Register shifts:** Does the formality level change between sections? Some shift is natural (a technical explanation in a conversational piece), but unmotivated shifts suggest patchwork composition.
- **Pronoun shifts:** Does the piece switch between "I," "we," and "you" inconsistently? Each choice implies a different relationship with the reader.
- **Confidence shifts:** Does the writer assert boldly in one section and hedge extensively in another without the subject matter justifying the shift?
- **Humor consistency:** If the piece opens with humor, does it sustain it? A humorous intro that gives way to a dry body signals that the intro was performing and the body is the real (less engaging) voice.

## Identifying a writer's natural strengths

Every writer has natural strengths — modes of expression they're best at. Identifying these is useful for both self-analysis ("lean into what works") and competitor analysis ("what makes their content effective").

### Common voice strengths

- **The analogist:** Makes complex things concrete through comparisons. Strengths: accessibility, memorability. Risk: analogies that obscure rather than illuminate.
- **The storyteller:** Makes abstract points through narrative. Strengths: engagement, emotional resonance. Risk: stories that don't earn their length or connect to the point.
- **The provocateur:** Leads with contrarian positions. Strengths: attention, differentiation, generates discussion. Risk: being contrarian for its own sake, positions that don't hold up.
- **The systems thinker:** Breaks complex topics into frameworks and models. Strengths: clarity, reusability, makes people smarter. Risk: over-systematizing things that resist it.
- **The practitioner:** Shares specific, first-hand experience. Strengths: credibility, specificity, usefulness. Risk: overgeneralizing from personal experience.
- **The curator:** Synthesizes others' ideas into new combinations. Strengths: breadth, context, connecting dots. Risk: adding insufficient original thinking.

These aren't exclusive — most good writers combine 2-3. But one usually dominates, and that's the strength to identify and lean into.

## Common voice problems

### Corporate committee voice

Every sentence has been reviewed by legal, marketing, and the CEO's assistant. All personality removed. All opinions hedged. The result is content that says nothing wrong and nothing interesting. Symptoms: passive voice, third-person-only, no specific examples, no admitted failures, every paragraph could be a press release.

### Trying-too-hard casual

The writer is performing informality. Forced humor, unnecessary slang, exclamation marks doing the work that good writing should do. The voice feels like a costume rather than a personality. The tell: remove all the casual markers and the content reads better, not worse.

### Inconsistent register

The intro is a LinkedIn post, the middle is a whitepaper, and the conclusion is a tweet. This usually happens when different sections were written at different times or by different people (including AI). The reader can't settle into a relationship with the writer because the writer keeps changing.

### The "helpful assistant" tone

Characteristic of AI-generated or AI-heavy content. Relentlessly helpful, scrupulously balanced, never wrong but also never interesting. The voice has no edges — nothing that could offend, surprise, or challenge. The content serves the reader like a waiter serves food: politely, competently, and without any personality that would distract from the meal. The problem is that in writing, personality IS the meal.
