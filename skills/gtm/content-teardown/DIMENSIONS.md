# DIMENSIONS.md

The ten analysis dimensions for a content teardown. For each dimension: what to look for, how to evaluate it, and what separates strong from weak.

---

## 1. Hook & title

What makes someone click, and what makes them keep reading past the first paragraph. The title is a contract — it promises something. The opening either honors or breaks that contract.

### What to look for

- **Title specificity:** Does the title promise something specific enough to filter — attracting the right reader and repelling the wrong one? "How We Cut Churn by 40% in 90 Days" filters. "Thoughts on Customer Retention" doesn't.
- **Title-to-content contract:** Does the body deliver what the title promised? A title that promises a framework had better contain a framework, not just observations.
- **First paragraph:** Does it earn the second paragraph? The reader's implicit question is "why should I keep reading?" — the opening must answer it in 2-3 sentences.
- **The "bar test":** If you described this post's topic to someone at a bar, would they lean in or check their phone? If they'd check their phone, the hook is missing.
- **Hook type:** What mechanism draws the reader in? Contrarian claim, specific result, unresolved tension, "you're doing X wrong," surprising data, a story that creates a question. Each has tradeoffs.

### Hooks that work vs. hooks that don't

**Work:** "We spent $400K on content marketing last year. Here's what we'd cut." (Specific, vulnerable, promises insider knowledge.) "Most pricing pages are optimized for the wrong metric." (Contrarian, specific enough to be interesting, implies the writer knows the right metric.) "I've reviewed 200 SaaS landing pages. Three patterns separate the ones that convert." (Credibility through volume, specific promise.)

**Don't work:** "In today's fast-paced digital landscape..." (Generic throat-clearing.) "The Ultimate Guide to Content Marketing." (Undifferentiated, makes promises the content rarely keeps.) "Why [Topic] Matters." (The reader already clicked — they know it matters. Tell them something they don't know.)

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** The title makes a specific promise. The first paragraph earns the next one. You know what you're going to learn and why this writer is the one to teach it. The title-to-content contract is honored.

**Adequate:** The title is clear but not compelling. The opening is competent but doesn't create urgency. You'd keep reading if you had nothing else to do, but you wouldn't bookmark it.

**Weak:** The title is generic or clickbait. The opening is throat-clearing — three paragraphs of context before the point. The title promises something the content doesn't deliver.

## 2. Structure

How the argument is organized — whether there's a throughline, whether the order is inevitable, whether each section earns the next.

### What to look for

- **Throughline:** Is there one argument, developed? Or is this a list of tangentially related points wearing a trenchcoat?
- **Section order:** Is the order inevitable — would rearranging sections break the argument? Or could you shuffle them without the reader noticing? Inevitable order is a sign of real structure.
- **Earning the next section:** Does each section create a question that the next section answers? Or does each section stand alone, connected only by proximity?
- **Opening-to-closing arc:** Does the piece move somewhere? Does the conclusion say something the introduction couldn't have said? If the conclusion is just a restatement of the introduction, the body didn't do any work.
- **Length justification:** Does every section earn its length? A 3,000-word post needs 3,000 words of insight, not 1,000 words of insight and 2,000 words of filler.

### Common structural problems

- **Throat-clearing intro:** Three paragraphs of context and qualifications before the point. The fix is usually to delete everything before the first interesting sentence.
- **The listicle disguised as an essay:** "Here are 7 things about X" where each item is a paragraph that doesn't connect to the others. Lists are fine — but call them lists. Don't pretend a list is an argument.
- **The post that ends when the writer ran out of things to say:** No conclusion, no synthesis, no "so what." The piece just... stops. The reader is left holding a bag of observations with no instruction on what to do with them.
- **The kitchen sink:** The writer had 5 ideas and put them all in one post instead of picking the best one and developing it fully. The result is 5 shallow points instead of 1 deep one.
- **Missing transitions:** Sections that feel like separate documents stapled together. The reader has to do the connecting work that the writer should have done.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** One clear argument, developed through sections that build on each other. The order is inevitable. The conclusion says something the introduction couldn't have said. Every section earns its length.

**Adequate:** The structure is logical but not tight. Sections are related but don't build. The order could be shuffled without major damage. The piece communicates but doesn't build momentum.

**Weak:** No throughline. Sections are disconnected. The piece reads like a first draft where the writer was figuring out what they think. The conclusion restates the introduction. Sections could be removed without the reader noticing.

## 3. Argument quality

Whether claims are earned — backed by evidence, experience, or logic — or merely asserted.

### What to look for

- **Claim inventory:** What claims does the piece make? List them. For each: is it backed by evidence, experience, or logic? Or is it just asserted?
- **Evidence type:** For each supported claim — what kind of evidence? Data, case study, personal experience, reasoning from principles, analogy, appeal to authority? Different types have different weights (see EVIDENCE-FRAMEWORK.md).
- **Counterargument handling:** Does the piece acknowledge the strongest objection to its thesis? Ignoring obvious counterarguments makes the writer look either naive or dishonest. Addressing them builds credibility.
- **Conclusion earned:** Is the conclusion supported by the body, or does it make a leap? The "so what" test: if you strip away the conclusion and ask "so what?" of the body, do you arrive at the same place the writer did?
- **Scope honesty:** Does the writer acknowledge the limits of their argument? "This worked for our team of 12; I don't know if it scales to 500" is more credible than implying universal applicability.

### The "so what" test — three levels deep

Level 1: "We reduced deploy time by 80%." So what?
Level 2: "Engineers ship features in hours instead of days." So what?
Level 3: "We can respond to customer feedback in the same week we hear it, which means our product-market fit feedback loop is 4x faster than competitors'."

Content that stops at Level 1 is a press release. Level 2 is a reasonable blog post. Level 3 is insight. Push every claim through this test to find where the real value lies.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** Claims are specific and supported. Evidence matches the weight of the claim. Counterarguments are addressed. The conclusion is earned by the body. The writer acknowledges scope limitations.

**Adequate:** Claims are reasonable but under-supported. Evidence exists but is thin — one example doing the work of three. Counterarguments are absent but not glaringly so. The conclusion is plausible but not inevitable.

**Weak:** Claims are asserted without evidence. The piece is a series of opinions presented as facts. Obvious counterarguments are ignored. The conclusion is a leap from the body. The writer implies universal applicability from limited experience.

## 4. Audience fit

Who this content is for, and whether the language, depth, and assumptions match that audience.

### What to look for

- **Implied reader:** Who is the content written for? What role, expertise level, situation? This is often different from who the writer thinks they're writing for.
- **Language match:** Does the vocabulary match the audience's expertise? Jargon signals insiders. Plain language signals broader reach. Neither is wrong — but mismatches are.
- **Assumption inventory:** What does the writer assume the reader already knows? Each assumption is a potential drop-off point for readers who don't share it.
- **The "expertise cursor":** Where does this content sit between "101 intro" and "deep expert content"? Is it consistent? Posts that oscillate between explaining basics and using advanced jargon are serving no one well.
- **Value match:** Does the content solve a problem the audience actually has, or a problem the writer wishes they had? The difference between "how to scale engineering teams" written for a 5-person startup vs. a 500-person company.

### Signs of audience mismatch

- Over-explaining basics to experts. ("As you may know, APIs are a way for software to communicate..." — if your reader doesn't know this, they're not the audience for your API design post.)
- Using jargon with beginners without defining it. The reader feels excluded rather than educated.
- Mixing audience levels. The post starts for beginners and ends for experts, or vice versa. No single reader feels served.
- Solving the wrong problem. Writing about enterprise scaling patterns for indie developers, or bootstrapping advice for venture-funded teams.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** The audience is clear from the first paragraph. Language, depth, and assumptions are consistent and appropriate. The content solves a problem this specific audience actually has. A reader from the target audience would feel "this was written for me."

**Adequate:** The audience is identifiable but the content doesn't fully commit to serving them. Some jargon goes unexplained or some concepts are over-explained. The content is useful but not targeted.

**Weak:** The audience is unclear or the content serves no specific reader well. Language oscillates between levels. The writer is talking to "everyone" and therefore no one. Or: the content solves a problem the audience doesn't have.

## 5. Readability

The mechanical qualities that determine whether the content is easy to read — sentence structure, paragraph density, scanning aids, and reading level.

### What to look for

- **Sentence length variation:** Monotonous long sentences exhaust. Monotonous short sentences bore. The rhythm should vary — a long sentence that builds complexity, then a short one that lands the point. Read a paragraph aloud: does it have rhythm, or does it drone?
- **Paragraph length:** For web content, 1-4 sentences per paragraph. Wall-of-text paragraphs signal academic writing or first-draft thinking. Single-sentence paragraphs used sparingly create emphasis; overused, they feel breathless.
- **Subheading frequency:** Every 200-300 words for web content. Subheadings serve scanners — and most readers scan before they read. A post without subheadings is asking the reader to trust that it's worth reading before they can verify.
- **Scanning aids:** Bold text, bullet lists, numbered steps, pull quotes — anything that lets a scanner extract the key points without reading every word. Used for emphasis: helpful. Used for decoration: noise.
- **Jargon density:** Count the terms a general reader wouldn't know. More than 2-3 per paragraph and you've lost the non-specialist. If the audience is specialists, jargon is fine — but it should be precise, not decorative.
- **Passive voice:** Occasional passive is fine. Habitual passive hides agency and reads as evasive. "Mistakes were made" vs. "We made mistakes." Count the instances — more than 20% passive voice is a signal.
- **Reading level:** Flesch-Kincaid is a rough signal, not a target. Most successful web content reads at 8th-10th grade level, even for expert audiences. Complexity should be in the ideas, not the syntax.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** Varied sentence rhythm. Short paragraphs. Frequent subheadings. Scanning aids used for emphasis, not decoration. Jargon appropriate to the audience. You can extract the key argument by scanning in 30 seconds and then choose to read deeply.

**Adequate:** Readable but not optimized. Paragraphs run a bit long. Subheadings exist but are infrequent. The content rewards careful reading but doesn't reward scanning. A reader who starts will finish, but a scanner might not start.

**Weak:** Wall-of-text paragraphs. No subheadings. No scanning aids. Monotonous sentence length. High jargon density without audience justification. The content is a chore to read regardless of how good the ideas are.

## 6. Voice & tone

Whether the content sounds like a human with a perspective, or like a committee, a template, or an AI.

### What to look for

- **Detectable point of view:** Does the writer have a stance, or are they summarizing what "some people think"? A point of view is the price of admission for content that matters.
- **Voice markers:** Specific word choices, sentence rhythm, humor style, what the writer assumes the reader knows, what they choose not to explain. These are fingerprints — they make the writing recognizable.
- **Tone consistency:** Does the tone stay consistent, or does it shift between sections? A post that starts irreverent and ends corporate has a voice problem.
- **Personality markers:** Specific opinions, idiosyncratic examples, unusual analogies, humor, self-deprecation, confidence, vulnerability. These are what make writing memorable.
- **AI voice tells:** The specific patterns that signal AI-generated or heavily AI-edited content (see VOICE-ANALYSIS.md for the full framework).

### Quick AI voice check

Does the content exhibit any of these patterns?
- Hedging: "It's important to note that..." "It's worth mentioning..."
- False balance: "On the other hand..." when there's no genuine counterpoint
- Generic transitions: "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally" as paragraph openers
- Empty superlatives: "incredibly powerful," "truly transformative," "game-changing"
- No personality markers: could have been written by anyone, about anything
- Bullet-point voice: every point starts with a bold keyword followed by a dash and explanation, in the same cadence

See VOICE-ANALYSIS.md for the complete detection framework and for evaluating voice quality beyond AI detection.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** The writer has a detectable voice and a clear point of view. You could identify the author without a byline. The tone is consistent. Personality markers make the content memorable. The writing sounds like a person who has thought about this, not a person who has researched this.

**Adequate:** The writing is clear and competent but not distinctive. It serves the content without adding personality. You wouldn't identify the author, but you wouldn't suspect AI either. Functional, not memorable.

**Weak:** The content sounds like a committee, a template, or an AI. No detectable point of view. Hedging and false balance substitute for opinion. Generic transitions connect generic paragraphs. The writing could have been produced by anyone — or anything — on any topic. OR: the voice is inconsistent, shifting between registers without reason.

## 7. Originality

Whether this content contributes something new — a new idea, framework, data point, or perspective — or restates what's already available elsewhere.

### What to look for

- **The "delete test":** If this post disappeared from the internet, what would be lost? If the answer is "nothing, because this information exists in 50 other posts," originality is low. Low originality isn't always a problem — good explainers for new audiences have value — but it should be identified, not disguised.
- **Unique contribution type:** What kind of originality, if any?
  - Original research or data (highest value — no one else has this)
  - Original framework or mental model (gives people a new way to think about something familiar)
  - Original opinion with argued defense (takes a stance and earns it)
  - Original synthesis of existing ideas (connects dots others haven't)
  - Restated conventional wisdom (lowest originality — has its place, but be honest about it)
- **The "so what's new" test:** After reading, can you complete the sentence "I didn't know that..." or "I hadn't thought about it that way because..."? If you can't, the content is restating rather than contributing.
- **Insight density:** How many genuinely new ideas per 1,000 words? A post with one strong original insight is more valuable than a post with ten restated observations.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** The content contributes something you can't find elsewhere — original data, a new framework, a defended contrarian position, or a synthesis that creates new understanding. After reading, you think differently about the topic.

**Adequate:** The content has a point of view and is well-expressed, but the core ideas are available elsewhere. It's a good version of existing thinking — well-organized, well-written, accessible — but not a new contribution. A solid explainer or summary.

**Weak:** The content restates conventional wisdom without adding a new angle, and it doesn't acknowledge that it's doing so. It reads like a Wikipedia summary with a byline. Or: the "unique insight" is obvious to anyone who's spent a week in the field.

## 8. Evidence quality

How well claims are supported — the quality, specificity, and honesty of the evidence presented.

### What to look for

- **Specificity:** Numbers vs. "many." Named companies vs. "leading brands." Dates and timeframes vs. "recently." Specific is credible; vague is suspicious.
- **Source quality:** First-hand experience, named sources with credentials, original data, published research with citations — vs. "studies show," "experts say," "it's well-known that." The evidence hierarchy in EVIDENCE-FRAMEWORK.md provides the full ranking.
- **Context for data:** Numbers without context are meaningless. "We grew 300%" — from what base? "Our NPS is 72" — what's the industry benchmark? Data needs a comparison point to mean anything.
- **Cherry-picking signals:** Does the evidence represent the full picture, or only the cases that support the thesis? All case studies are successes? All data trends upward? No failures mentioned? That's not evidence — it's marketing.
- **Honesty about limitations:** Does the writer acknowledge what the evidence doesn't prove? "This worked for us but we had unusual advantages" is more credible than "this will work for you too."

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** Claims are backed by specific, verifiable evidence. Data has context. Sources are named. The writer acknowledges limitations. You could fact-check the key claims, and they'd hold up.

**Adequate:** Evidence exists but is thin. One example supports a broad claim. Numbers are present but lack context. Sources are referenced but not specific enough to verify. The claims are plausible but under-supported.

**Weak:** Claims are asserted without evidence or supported by vague authority ("studies show," "it's widely known"). Data is cherry-picked or context-free. The writer presents opinion as fact. You couldn't fact-check the key claims because there's nothing specific enough to check.

## 9. SEO/AEO alignment

How well the content is structured for search engine discovery and AI citation — heading structure, search intent matching, and citability.

### What to look for

- **Heading structure:** Does the heading hierarchy serve as both a scannable outline and a search-intent map? H1 should be the topic. H2s should be the key subtopics someone would search for. H3s provide structure within subtopics.
- **Search intent match:** What query would lead someone to this content? Does the content actually answer that query? A post titled "How to Price Your SaaS" that's really about the author's pricing journey has a search-intent mismatch.
- **Citability:** Could an AI extract a specific paragraph as a standalone answer? Self-contained paragraphs with clear topic sentences are citable. Paragraphs that depend on three previous paragraphs for context are not.
- **Freshness signals:** Dates, "updated" indicators, references to current events or recent data. Content without freshness signals gets deprioritized by both search engines and AI systems.
- **Entity clarity:** Does the content make clear what or who it's about? Named products, specific concepts, defined terms — these help both search engines and AI systems understand and categorize the content.

Cross-reference seo-aeo/CONTENT-FRAMEWORK.md for the full framework on content structure, E-E-A-T signals, and AI-optimized content patterns.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** Heading structure maps to search intent. Key paragraphs are self-contained and citable. Freshness signals are present. The content would rank for relevant queries and could be cited by AI systems with proper attribution.

**Adequate:** Basic SEO hygiene is present — headings exist, the topic is clear — but the content isn't optimized for extraction or citation. AI would have to work to pull a clean answer from this content.

**Weak:** No heading structure, or headings that are clever rather than descriptive ("The Big Reveal" instead of "Results"). Content is not structured for search intent. No freshness signals. AI systems would struggle to extract a useful, self-contained answer.

## 10. CTA & purpose

What the reader is supposed to do, think, or feel after reading — and whether the content earns that response.

### What to look for

- **Stated or implied purpose:** What's the goal? Educate, persuade, inspire action, build authority, generate leads, start a conversation? Every piece of content has a purpose, even if the writer hasn't articulated it.
- **CTA presence:** Is there an explicit ask — subscribe, try the product, read the next post, share, implement the advice? Or does the piece end without direction?
- **CTA placement:** Where does the ask come? At the end? Inline? Multiple times? A CTA before the content has earned the reader's trust feels premature. A CTA after a strong piece feels natural.
- **Earned vs. unearned:** Does the content quality justify the ask? A 500-word post of restated advice that ends with "subscribe for more insights" hasn't earned the subscription. A deep, original analysis that ends with "we publish one of these monthly" has.
- **Emotional landing:** How does the reader feel at the end? Energized, informed, challenged, inspired, frustrated, indifferent? The emotional landing should match the purpose. A motivational post that ends with dense caveats undercuts itself.

### The content-CTA relationship

A weak post with a strong CTA feels manipulative — the reader senses they were lured to a sales pitch. A strong post with no CTA is a missed opportunity — the reader is primed to act but given no direction. A strong post with a matched CTA feels natural — the content demonstrated value, and the CTA offers more of it.

### How to evaluate

**Strong:** The purpose is clear. The CTA (explicit or implicit) matches the content's value. The reader knows what to do next and wants to do it. The emotional landing reinforces the purpose.

**Adequate:** The purpose is discernible but the CTA is weak or absent. The reader finishes with understanding but no direction. The content works as a standalone piece but doesn't connect to anything larger.

**Weak:** No discernible purpose — the post exists because the content calendar said "publish something." The CTA is disconnected from the content (a thought-leadership piece that ends with a product pitch). Or: the CTA is aggressive relative to the value delivered. The reader feels tricked rather than served.
