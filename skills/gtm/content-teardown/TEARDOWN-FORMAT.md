# TEARDOWN-FORMAT.md

The deliverable template for a content teardown. This is the structure of the final document — not a checklist to fill in mechanically, but a format that ensures the analysis is useful and scannable.

---

## Document structure

### Verdict (2-3 sentences)

The content's essential character in three sentences or fewer. Lead with the strongest move and the biggest miss. End with who this content is really for (which may differ from who it claims to be for).

**Example verdicts:**

> This post's strongest move is the specificity of its evidence — every claim is backed by a named customer, a dollar amount, or a date, which gives it the credibility that most "lessons learned" posts lack. Its biggest miss is the structure: the argument is buried under four paragraphs of origin story that only the writer's friends will read. This is for mid-stage founders who've already tried the obvious growth playbooks and are looking for non-obvious ones.

> The strongest move here is the contrarian framing — "your pricing page is lying to you" creates tension that carries the reader through 2,000 words. The biggest miss is that the post never resolves the tension: it identifies the problem but offers only vague gestures toward a solution, which feels like a bait-and-switch. This is for SaaS founders who are price-sensitive about their own pricing, but it will frustrate readers who came for the "how."

**What makes a good verdict:**
- Names the specific strong/weak dimension (not "the writing is good" — "the evidence is specific, which matters because Y")
- Identifies the real audience (which might differ from the stated audience)
- Creates a mental model the reader can carry through the rest of the document

### Gut reactions

The instinctive responses from the first read — before analysis kicked in. These are the reader experience data that analysis alone can't generate.

- **Title reaction:** Would you click this? What did you expect to read?
- **First paragraph:** Did it earn the next one? Where were you after 30 seconds?
- **Interest peak:** Where did the content grab you most? What made that moment work?
- **Interest drop:** Where did you first skim or lose focus? What triggered it?
- **The takeaway:** What's the one thing you'd remember tomorrow?

This section should be 4-6 bullet points maximum. It's a snapshot, not an essay.

### Dimension scores

A table for at-a-glance orientation. Scores are strong/adequate/weak. The one-line note justifies the score — it's not a summary of the full analysis, just enough to orient the reader.

| Dimension | Score | Note |
|-----------|-------|------|
| Hook & title | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| Structure | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| Argument quality | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| Audience fit | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| Readability | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| Voice & tone | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| Originality | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| Evidence quality | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| SEO/AEO alignment | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |
| CTA & purpose | strong/adequate/weak | [one line] |

**Scoring guidance:**
- **Strong** — This dimension is a clear asset. The writer made intentional, effective decisions here. Worth studying.
- **Adequate** — Functional, unremarkable. Neither helps nor hurts. Standard for the category.
- **Weak** — Noticeable problems that likely affect the content's impact. Specific improvement opportunities exist.

Most dimensions for most content will score adequate. Strong or weak should be notable and explained.

### What works (2-4 items)

Things the content does notably well — with quotes from the content itself. These are the lessons worth studying regardless of the overall quality. For each:

- **What:** The specific thing that works (not "good writing" — "the transition in paragraph 4 reframes the reader's assumption, which makes the next section feel necessary rather than additive")
- **Why it works:** The underlying principle
- **The evidence:** A direct quote from the content that demonstrates it

### What doesn't (2-4 items)

Things that are weak or missing, with what better would look like. These are not complaints — they're constructive observations with specific alternatives. For each:

- **What's happening:** The current state (specific, with a quote if possible)
- **Why it's a problem:** What the reader experiences as a result
- **What better looks like:** A specific alternative — not a vague "make it stronger" but a concrete direction ("add the specific conversion rate from paragraph 2's case study to back this claim")

### Line-level notes (if the user's own content)

Specific, actionable edits for improving the draft. These are the surgical recommendations — the things a skilled editor would flag.

Format:

- **Cut:** [quote the specific text] — reason it should go
- **Strengthen:** [quote the claim] — what evidence or specificity would make this land
- **Restructure:** [describe the move] — why this order would serve the argument better
- **Rewrite:** [quote the weak sentence] — what's wrong with it and the direction for a fix (not a replacement sentence — let the writer maintain voice)

Be selective. 5-8 line-level notes are more useful than 20. Focus on the changes that would have the biggest impact.

### Transferable lessons (if someone else's content)

When the user is tearing down content they didn't write, extract what's structurally useful for their own work. For each lesson:

- **The pattern:** What the writer does (structure, not surface — the technique, not the topic)
- **Why it's transferable:** What makes it work independent of this specific content
- **How to adapt it:** What would change when applied to the user's context and voice

Only include this section when analyzing someone else's content. Omit for self-analysis.

## Tone guidance

Teardowns should be honest but constructive. The goal is to make the content's decisions legible — to explain why something works or doesn't — not to score points for cleverness or demonstrate superiority.

**Do:**
- "The claim in paragraph 3 — 'most companies get this wrong' — has no evidence. Which companies? How specifically do they get it wrong? Adding one named example would transform this from assertion to argument."
- "The opening story works because it creates a specific, unresolved question ('how did a 4-person team outship a 40-person team?') that the reader needs answered."
- "The voice is consistent throughout — direct, slightly irreverent, confident without being arrogant. This is the writer's biggest asset."

**Don't:**
- "This paragraph is weak."
- "The writer clearly doesn't understand their audience."
- "This reads like ChatGPT wrote it." (Instead: "This paragraph exhibits several AI-generation tells — the 'it's important to note' opener, the false-balance pivot, and the absence of any personal perspective. See the voice dimension for specific markers.")

Specificity is respect. Quoting the content when critiquing it shows you read carefully and care about getting the feedback right.

---

## Length guidance

A content teardown should be 500-1,000 words in the final document. Long enough to be substantive, short enough to be read. The verdict and dimension scores should orient a reader in 30 seconds. The full document should take 3-5 minutes to read.

Line-level notes (for the user's own content) can add 200-400 words beyond the base teardown. Keep them surgical — each note should be one or two sentences.

If the analysis for any single dimension exceeds 150 words in the final document, you're going too deep for the format. The teardown is a map, not the territory. Save deep-dives for follow-up conversation.
